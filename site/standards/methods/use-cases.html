Use case method

methods/use-cases.md · Edition a5a7eee of avd-assess, 2026-10-21. What changed

Who uses the estate, for what, and what that means it should be. Collection says what is there and assessment judges it against published guidance; this method supplies the other half of "should be": the personas, their apps, their performance and experience needs, and their security and compliance needs. It is the analysis side of discovery, the second stage of an assessment ([G1]). avd-current-state checks a use case record against what it collects; nothing here needs a layer above it.

Method

  1. Start from the default, then test it. Assume one persona with the proof of concept's assumptions (one shared pool, multi-session compatible apps, light density, low latency) and look for what breaks each one ([1, assumptions], [1, assumptions-rarely-all-true], [2, assumption-validation]). The landing zone builds that default today ([O1]).
  2. Interview before you group. Run the discovery questions by role; structured answers go to the intake, narrative stays in the operator's private evidence ([G1]).
  3. Group users into personas by the eight published criteria: dedicated or pooled, density, performance, GPU, where they work from, business function, user count and peak sessions ([2, persona-criteria]). Fold any group under 10 users into its nearest neighbor ([G2]).
  4. Start each persona from the library: one base persona and any overlays in use-cases.yaml, which give the workload type, starting values, risks to ask about and what would move the persona elsewhere ([G3]). The workload type stays a prior to be measured, not a fact ([3, workload-types], [3, estimates-only], [O2]). Where the persona's main app publishes its own tiers, use those ([4, app-specific-tiers]).
  5. Ask the app questions per persona: installed apps and Microsoft 365 decide the image, multi-session compatibility decides pooled or personal, and back-end latency can decide the region or block the persona until the app moves ([2, application-questions], [5, legacy-apps-personal], [5, app-remediation-blocks]). Where personas share a pool, deliver their different apps by assignment, not by separate images ([6, per-user-per-host-pool]).
  6. Record security and compliance needs per persona: who may share a host, which data controls the session must enforce, and how critical the work is, which sets reliability targets before anything else ([G2], [7, RE:04]).
  7. Write each persona as a structured record with no names: enums, counts, public regions and clock times ([G2]). Absent records mean the default from step 1.
  8. Map each record to the design decisions it informs, one persona to one host pool configuration ([2, persona-to-host-pool], [G2]).
  9. Check each record against what is there: the deployed host pool type, application groups, sizes, session limits and region from current-state facts, and, once measured, concurrency, per-user demand, profile size and latency from the users' side ([G2], [8, utilization-for-scaling], [O3]). Each field gets pass, warn, gap or unknown; a model never decides which ([G4]).
  10. Plan in persona iterations, one persona and host pool at a time, so the business absorbs one change at once ([5, persona-iterations]).
  11. Repeat when use changes, not only on the assessment cadence: a new team, a new app or a move to new regions reopens the record ([9, milestones]).

Where it fits

Assessment stage This method's part
1. Collection none; it reads the current-state facts collection writes
2. Discovery the persona, app and control questions ([G1])
3. Analysis steps 3 to 9: the record, the decisions it informs, and the check against what is there
4. Review each gap or warn is a finding a person reviews against the private narrative
5. Recommendations step 10: persona iterations become the order of the plan

The second brain can keep the record over the estate's life as the reason behind each decision. That is an optional reader: the record, this method and avd-current-state's checks work the same without it.

Not yet known

  • The record isn't in the intake contract or the fact register yet; it is proposed in [G2], and whether it records which library entries a persona started from is open ([G3]).
  • Whether published workload types agree with measured per-user demand hasn't been tested on real users ([O2]).
  • No published source covers personas after the Framework's AVD pages leave Learn ([2, deprecated]); the pins keep the citations working.

Our observations

What our own runs showed about a cited claim. Evidence links are public and pinned.

  1. [O1]agrees landing-zone-one-persona, on caf-avd-poc#assumptions, 2026-10-20. The landing zone builds the proof of concept's shape. Its sizing keeps a list of host pools but allows one entry today, sized from one workload's users per vCPU, so every user is one persona until a use case assessment says otherwise.
  2. [O2]not yet tested users-per-vcpu-adopted, on session-host-sizing#multisession-users-per-vcpu, 2026-10-20. Adopted as the landing zone's automatic sizing input, and its preflight warns above 6 sessions per vCPU. No real users have been measured against it, so the numbers are still Microsoft's prior, not our evidence.
  3. [O3]extends region-latency-from-the-user, on avd-prerequisites#rtt-under-150, 2026-10-20. Latency measured from Cloud Shell says nothing about users, because Cloud Shell runs in an Azure datacenter. Measure round-trip time from the user's device, against endpoints verified to be regional.
